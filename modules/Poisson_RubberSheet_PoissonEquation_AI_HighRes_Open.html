Poisson Equation: Rubber-Sheet Potential Surface φ(x,y)
Poisson Rubber-Sheet Metaphor Guide
Main Goal

Poisson equation: source charge → curvature → slope → potential

Principle: a source distribution \(\rho(x,y)\) does not directly become the potential. It first determines the curvature of the potential surface through Poisson's equation.

Rubber-sheet metaphor: source charges push or pull the sheet; the sheet height represents potential \(\phi\); the local bending represents \(\nabla^2\phi\); the slope represents the field-like quantity \(-\nabla\phi\).

Equation map: \(\rho(x,y)\rightarrow \nabla^2\phi(x,y)\rightarrow -\nabla\phi(x,y)\rightarrow \phi(x,y)\).

Project 1

Source charge and curvature

  1. Load Opposite charge sheets.
  2. Look at the heat map of \(\rho(x,y)\) and the curvature map \(\nabla^2\phi\).
  3. Explain why the potential surface bends where the source charge exists.
  4. State the local rule: large \(|\rho|\) creates large curvature magnitude.
Project 2

Sign of \(\rho\) and sign of curvature

Compare Single positive source and Single negative source. The goal is to connect source sign to whether the sheet bends upward or downward in this chosen sign convention.

Key idea: Poisson's equation fixes the sign relation between \(\rho\) and \(\nabla^2\phi\): \(\nabla^2\phi=-\rho/\epsilon\).

Project 3

Slope, gradient, and field direction

Turn on the arrows. They show the negative gradient direction \(-\nabla\phi\). Students should distinguish height \(\phi\), slope \(\nabla\phi\), and curvature \(\nabla^2\phi\).

Project 4

Superposition of multiple sources

Load a dipole, a same-sign pair, and a quadrupole. The total surface is the combined contribution of all sources.

Key idea: for a linear Poisson problem, source contributions add: \(\rho=\rho_1+\rho_2+\cdots\), so the resulting potential is a superposition.

Project 5

Permittivity controls curvature strength

Move the permittivity slider \(\epsilon\). For the same source charge, larger \(\epsilon\) reduces the curvature magnitude.

Key relation: \(\nabla^2\phi=-\rho/\epsilon\). The material parameter controls how strongly the sheet bends for a given source.

Project 6

Smoothing, localization, and numerical intuition

Move the smoothing slider \(\sigma\). A small \(\sigma\) makes a localized source; a larger \(\sigma\) spreads the source over a broader region.

Learning goal: see why localized sources produce sharp curvature features and why numerical simulations often use smoothed sources.

Project 7

From rubber-sheet observation to Poisson's equation

  1. Observe where the sheet bends most strongly.
  2. Compare that location with the source-charge map \(\rho(x,y)\).
  3. Use the curvature map to identify \(\nabla^2\phi\).
  4. Use arrows/centerline slope to identify \(-\nabla\phi\).
  5. Write the complete relationship: \(\nabla^2\phi=-\rho/\epsilon\).
Project 8

Misconceptions and exit reflection

Students finish by explaining what the rubber sheet represents and what it does not represent.
