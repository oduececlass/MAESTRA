Intrinsic semiconductor
\[ n=p=n_i,\qquad n_i=\sqrt{N_C N_V}\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_g}{2kT}\right) \]

Pure material has equal thermally generated electrons and holes.

Doped semiconductor
\[ n\approx N_D,\quad p=\frac{n_i^2}{N_D}, \qquad p\approx N_A,\quad n=\frac{n_i^2}{N_A} \]

Doping makes one carrier type the majority carrier and suppresses the minority carrier.

Fermi-Dirac probability
\[ f(E)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(\frac{E-E_F}{kT}\right)} \]

The Fermi level controls the probability that a state is occupied.

Carrier concentration from probability
\[ n=\int_{E_C}^{\infty} g_C(E)f(E)\,dE, \qquad p=\int_{-\infty}^{E_V}g_V(E)[1-f(E)]\,dE \]

Carrier concentration is density of states times occupation probability, integrated over energy.

The central idea of basic semiconductor physics is simple: available energy states tell us where electrons could go, and probability tells us how likely those states are occupied.

\[ \text{carrier concentration}=\int(\text{density of available states})(\text{occupation probability})\,dE \]

For electrons in the conduction band, the concentration is found from the product \(g_C(E)f(E)\). For holes in the valence band, the concentration is found from \(g_V(E)[1-f(E)]\).

Density of states
How many quantum states are available at energy \(E\).
Occupation probability
The probability that a state at energy \(E\) is occupied by an electron.
Fermi level
The energy reference that controls the occupation probability.

Learning path

  1. Start with thermal energy \(kT\) and the Boltzmann factor.
  2. Use probability to understand why high-energy states are less likely occupied.
  3. Introduce Fermi-Dirac occupation for electrons.
  4. Multiply occupation probability by density of states.
  5. Integrate to obtain \(n\), \(p\), and \(n_i\).
  6. Use charge neutrality to locate the Fermi level in doped semiconductors.

2. Thermal Energy and the Boltzmann Factor

At temperature \(T\), particles exchange energy with the thermal environment. The natural energy scale is \(kT\), where \(k\) is Boltzmann's constant.

\[ kT \approx 0.0259\ \text{eV at }T=300\ \text{K} \]

If a particle must gain an energy \(\Delta E\), the relative probability of that thermal excitation is approximately the Boltzmann factor:

\[ P(\Delta E)\propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta E}{kT}\right) \]
Probability meaning: every additional energy cost of about \(kT\) multiplies the probability by \(e^{-1}\approx0.37\). A gap of \(10kT\) is much less likely than a gap of \(1kT\).

3. Fermi-Dirac Function

Electrons are fermions, so each quantum state can hold only a limited number of electrons. The probability that an electron state at energy \(E\) is occupied is the Fermi-Dirac function:

\[ f(E)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(\frac{E-E_F}{kT}\right)} \]

At \(E=E_F\), the probability is always \(f(E_F)=1/2\). Below \(E_F\), states are likely occupied. Above \(E_F\), states are less likely occupied.

Important correction: the Fermi level is not a physical band by itself. It is an energy reference that controls electron occupation probability.

4. Boltzmann Approximation

When an energy state is several \(kT\) above the Fermi level, the exponential term in the Fermi-Dirac denominator is much larger than 1. Then the Fermi-Dirac function becomes the Boltzmann approximation:

\[ E-E_F \gg kT \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(E)\approx \exp\!\left(-\frac{E-E_F}{kT}\right) \]

This approximation is the reason carrier concentration formulas often contain exponential terms. In nondegenerate semiconductors, the Fermi level is far enough from the band edge that Boltzmann statistics work well.

Exact probability
\(f(E)=1/[1+\exp((E-E_F)/kT)]\)
Approximate probability
\(f(E)\approx\exp[-(E-E_F)/kT]\)

5. Density of States and Occupation

The density of states tells us how many allowed electron states exist per unit volume per unit energy. In a 3D parabolic band, the conduction-band density of states increases like a square root above \(E_C\).

\[ g_C(E)\propto \sqrt{E-E_C},\qquad E\ge E_C \]

The electron concentration is not just the density of states and not just the probability. It is the product integrated over energy:

\[ n=\int_{E_C}^{\infty} g_C(E)f(E)\,dE \]

For holes, use empty-state probability in the valence band:

\[ p=\int_{-\infty}^{E_V} g_V(E)[1-f(E)]\,dE \]

6. Carrier Concentration Calculation

Under the nondegenerate approximation, the concentration formulas become compact exponentials:

\[ n=N_C\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_C-E_F}{kT}\right) \]
\[ p=N_V\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_F-E_V}{kT}\right) \]

These formulas show the strongest lesson: a small movement of \(E_F\) changes carrier concentration exponentially.

Probability interpretation: \(N_C\) and \(N_V\) summarize the number of thermally accessible states, while the exponential terms summarize the probability of occupying those states.

Electrons: count states near \(E_C\), then weight them by the probability that those states are occupied.
Step 1 — exact carrier counting
\[ n=\int_{E_C}^{\infty} g_C(E)f(E)\,dE \]

Exact counting starts from the product of available states and occupation probability.

Step 2 — use the Fermi-Dirac probability
\[ f(E)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(\frac{E-E_F}{kT}\right)} \]

The occupation probability becomes small high above \(E_F\).

Step 3 — Boltzmann approximation
\[ f(E)\approx\exp\!\left(-\frac{E-E_F}{kT}\right) \]

When the relevant band edge is several \(kT\) away from \(E_F\), the exact Fermi-Dirac function simplifies to an exponential.

Step 4 — collect the band-shape integral
\[ n=N_C\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_C-E_F}{kT}\right) \]
\[ n=n_i\exp\!\left(\frac{E_F-E_i}{kT}\right) \]

The DOS integral is summarized by \(N_C\), leaving the exponential probability factor visible.

7. Intrinsic Semiconductor and \(n_i\)

An intrinsic semiconductor has no intentional dopant atoms. At thermal equilibrium, electrons and holes are generated in pairs, so their concentrations are equal:

\[ n=p=n_i \]

Multiplying the nondegenerate formulas for \(n\) and \(p\) gives the mass-action relation:

\[ np=n_i^2 \]

The intrinsic carrier concentration depends strongly on bandgap and temperature:

\[ n_i=\sqrt{N_C N_V}\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_g}{2kT}\right) \]
The factor \(E_g/2\) appears because creating one electron-hole pair requires crossing the bandgap, while the intrinsic Fermi level lies near the middle of the gap.

8. Fermi Level Meaning and Position

The Fermi level \(E_F\) is the energy where the electron occupation probability is 50%. More importantly, it is the parameter that makes the total charge balance correctly.

\[ f(E_F)=\frac{1}{2} \]

For intrinsic material, the Fermi level is close to the intrinsic level \(E_i\). For n-type material, \(E_F\) moves upward toward \(E_C\). For p-type material, \(E_F\) moves downward toward \(E_V\).

Intrinsic
\(E_F\approx E_i\), \(n=p=n_i\)
n-type
\(E_F\) closer to \(E_C\), \(n\gg p\)
p-type
\(E_F\) closer to \(E_V\), \(p\gg n\)

9. Doped Semiconductors

Doping intentionally adds impurity atoms that donate electrons or accept electrons. In a simple fully ionized, nondegenerate semiconductor:

n-type donor doping
\[ n\approx N_D,\qquad p=\frac{n_i^2}{N_D} \]
p-type acceptor doping
\[ p\approx N_A,\qquad n=\frac{n_i^2}{N_A} \]

The majority carrier concentration increases with doping, while the minority carrier concentration decreases through the mass-action relation \(np=n_i^2\).

Charge neutrality

The exact Fermi level is found by enforcing charge neutrality:

\[ p+N_D^+=n+N_A^- \]

For most introductory problems at room temperature with full ionization, this reduces to simple majority-carrier approximations.

10. Calculation Workflow and Practice

Recommended calculation workflow

  1. Identify \(T\), \(E_g\), \(N_C\), \(N_V\), and doping.
  2. Calculate or estimate \(n_i\).
  3. Use doping to determine the majority carrier.
  4. Use \(np=n_i^2\) to find the minority carrier.
  5. Locate the Fermi level using \(E_F-E_i=kT\ln(n/n_i)\) or \(E_i-E_F=kT\ln(p/n_i)\).
\[ E_F-E_i=kT\ln\!\left(\frac{n}{n_i}\right), \qquad E_i-E_F=kT\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{n_i}\right) \]

Practice problems

Problem 1: Boltzmann factor

At 300 K, estimate the relative probability of a 0.20 eV excitation.

\[ kT=0.0259\ \text{eV},\qquad \exp(-0.20/0.0259)\approx 4.4\times10^{-4} \]
Problem 2: n-type concentration

For silicon with \(n_i=10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) and \(N_D=10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), estimate \(n\) and \(p\).

\[ n\approx10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3},\qquad p=\frac{10^{20}}{10^{16}}=10^4\ \text{cm}^{-3} \]

For \(n/n_i=10^6\) at 300 K, estimate \(E_F-E_i\).

\[ E_F-E_i=kT\ln(10^6)=0.0259(13.82)\approx0.36\ \text{eV} \]
